Car ownership in Japan is structurally different from the US. The sticker price of a Japanese-brand car is roughly on par with the US, but three Japan-specific systems — the biennial *shaken* inspection, the mandatory parking certificate, and the *kei* vehicle class — shape the real cost of ownership in ways that aren't obvious coming from the US. Understanding these upfront prevents expensive surprises.

## The Japanese Cost Structure vs. the US

| Cost | Japan | US |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price (domestic brands) | Roughly on par with US | — |
| Annual registration | ¥10,800 (kei) – ¥110,000 (large engine), paid in May | \$30–\$200/yr depending on state |
| Biennial vehicle inspection | ¥60,000–¥200,000 (*shaken*, every 2 years) | \$10–\$50 annual/biennial safety check |
| Compulsory liability insurance | Bundled into *shaken* (≈¥17,650 per 24 months) | Purchased separately |
| Parking | Must prove off-street space before registering | No requirement |
| Personal property tax on vehicle | None in most prefectures | Varies by state |
| Consumption tax on purchase | 10% | Sales tax (varies by state) |

### Shaken (車検) — the biggest structural difference

*Shaken* is Japan's compulsory motor-vehicle inspection system. A new car's first shaken is due 3 years after first registration, then every 2 years (commercial vehicles annually). It is far more rigorous and expensive than a US safety check because it bundles:

- **Statutory fees (法定費用)** — the same everywhere: automobile weight tax, compulsory liability insurance (*jibaiseki*) for 24 months, and an inspection stamp fee. For a typical 1.0–1.5-ton car these total roughly ¥44,000–¥45,000; kei cars pay about ¥26,000–¥27,000.
- **Service/maintenance charges** — vary by provider:

| Provider | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| User shaken (DIY at government inspection center) | Barely above statutory fees |
| Specialist chains (Autobacs, Yellow Hat, dedicated shaken shops) | ¥60,000–¥80,000 |
| Franchised dealer | ¥100,000–¥200,000 with bundled maintenance |

As of April 2025, shaken can be completed up to 2 months before expiry without losing days from the next cycle. Driving on expired shaken is a serious offense.

When buying used, always check how much shaken is remaining. A car sold with **fresh shaken (車検付き)** is worth more than one with shaken about to expire — factor this into price negotiation.

### Parking certificate (車庫証明) — required before registration

Unlike the US, a car cannot be registered until you prove to the police that you have an off-street parking space within 2 km of your registered address. This is a consequence of Japan's nationwide ban on overnight street parking.

- Apply at the police station covering your address; fee is roughly ¥2,100.
- Turnaround is about one week; police may physically inspect the space.
- If a dealer handles it, add roughly ¥10,000–¥30,000 in service fees.
- Kei cars are exempt from this requirement in many municipalities, but designated urban areas in the Kanto region generally do require it — confirm with the local police station for your specific address.
- Falsifying the parking address (*shako tobashi*) is a criminal offense with fines up to ¥200,000.

### Kei cars (軽自動車) — a class with no US equivalent

Kei cars are legally defined micro-vehicles: max 3.4m length, 1.48m width, 2.0m height, 660cc engine, 64 PS. They wear yellow plates and made up about 36.5% of new vehicle sales in Japan in 2025.

| Feature | Kei car | Standard (white-plate) car |
|---|---|---|
| Annual automobile tax | ¥10,800 flat (¥12,900 after 13 years) | ¥25,000–¥110,000 by engine size |
| Shaken cost | Lower | Higher |
| Parking certificate | Exempt in many areas | Required in most urban areas |
| Highway comfort | Limited | Better for longer trips |
| Crash performance | Lower | Higher |

A kei car is practical as a city/second car in suburban Kanto. For a family doing regular highway trips, a compact hybrid or hybrid minivan is a more comfortable primary vehicle.

### Ongoing taxes

**Automobile tax (自動車税種別割)** is paid annually in May, based on engine displacement:

| Displacement | Registered before Oct 1, 2019 | Registered on/after Oct 1, 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| ≤1,000cc | ¥29,500 | ¥25,000 |
| 1,000–1,500cc | ¥34,500 | ¥30,500 |
| 1,500–2,000cc | ¥39,500 | ¥36,000 |
| 2,000–2,500cc | ¥45,000 | ¥43,500 |
| 2,500–3,000cc | ¥51,000 | ¥50,000 |
| 3,000–3,500cc | ¥58,000 | ¥57,000 |
| >6,000cc | ¥111,000 | ¥110,000 |

**Weight tax (自動車重量税)** is paid as a 2-year block at each shaken — roughly ¥4,100 per 0.5 ton per year for a standard car (¥24,600 for two years on a 1.0–1.5t car). Hybrids and EVs get reductions; cars over 13 years old pay surcharges.

**Environmental performance tax (環境性能割)** was abolished effective March 31, 2026. Vehicles registered on or after April 1, 2026 pay ¥0 on this tax.

**Older-car surcharge:** gasoline cars over 13 years old pay roughly 15% more automobile tax (20% for kei). This is why Japanese cars are frequently sold or exported before that threshold.

## The Market

### Japanese brands dominate

Japanese brands were approximately 94% of sales in H1 2025. Toyota alone held roughly 30% of the full passenger-car market. The major domestic makers — Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Suzuki, Daihatsu, Mazda, Subaru, Mitsubishi — all sell here, but their Japanese lineups differ from US versions:

- Kei cars (Honda N-Box, Suzuki Spacia, Daihatsu Tanto) don't exist in the US market.
- Many minivans and tall compacts (Toyota Alphard, Noah/Voxy, Honda Freed, Nissan Serena) are JDM-only or differently-named.
- The best-selling car in Japan in 2025 was the Honda N-Box (kei); the best-selling standard car was the Toyota Yaris.

### Foreign brands carry a premium

Imports were 5.2% of the market in H1 2025. The category is German-dominated — Mercedes-Benz, BMW, VW, Audi. American brands are marginal (Jeep was the only US brand in the top 10 in 2024). European luxury cars cost noticeably more in Japan than in the US — a Mercedes C-Class starts around ¥8M–¥9M in Japan vs. ~\$48,000 in the US.

### The used market is transparent and good value

Japan is the world's largest used-car export market. Steep early depreciation (a 3-year-old car has typically lost ~40% of its value) and strict maintenance culture make used cars an excellent value — better relative value than the equivalent US used car.

Dealers source stock from physical auctions (USS, TAA, JU, CAA, AUCNET). Every auctioned car receives an **auction sheet (出品票)** from a certified inspector with:

- A **numeric/letter grade**: S or 6 = like new, down to 1; 4/4.5 = clean typical car; R/RA = repair history.
- A separate **accident/repair history field (修復歴, あり/なし)** — this flags structural repairs only (frame rails, pillars, floor pan, firewall), not bolt-on panels like doors or bumpers. Always check both the grade and the repair-history flag independently.

This is more transparent than the US market, where you rely on Carfax and a private mechanic.

**Where to shop:**

**Typical price ranges (2025–2026):**

|---|---|

| New compact (Yaris/Fit/Note) | ¥2.0–3.0M |

## Powertrain: Hybrid vs. EV

Hybrids are the right default choice for most buyers relocating from the US.

| Factor | Hybrid | Full BEV |
|---|---|---|
| Market share (H1 2025) | ~33.8% of new sales | ~1.3% of new sales |
| Home charging required | No | Effectively yes |
| Public charging network | N/A | Thin; ~30,000–31,600 points nationwide, ~8,200 fast |
| Fast-charge standard | N/A | CHAdeMO (differs from US CCS/NACS) |
| Purchase subsidy (CEV) | None | Up to ¥1,300,000 (from Jan 1, 2026) |
| Apartment charging | Not needed | Difficult; infrastructure still maturing |
| Fuel savings vs. gasoline | Significant | Maximum |

Japan's charging infrastructure is thinner than it appears — only 40% of Japanese EV users think public charging is fast enough (vs. 60% global average, per Roland Berger EV Charging Index 2025). Most Japanese apartments do not offer home charging. Hybrids fit short urban commutes without any behavior change.

If dedicated home (off-street) charging is available, a BEV or PHEV becomes viable and qualifies for the CEV subsidy. Kei EVs (Nissan Sakura, Mitsubishi eK X EV) and some imports (BYD Dolphin, Hyundai Inster) are the most affordable BEV options; imported BEVs averaged about ¥7.37M in H1 2025.

**CEV subsidy caps (from January 1, 2026):**

| Vehicle type | National subsidy cap | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| BEV (Battery Electric Vehicle) | ¥1,300,000 | Purely electric — no combustion engine. Charged from an external outlet or public charger. Examples: Nissan Leaf, Tesla Model 3, BYD Dolphin. |
| PHEV (Plug-in Hybrid) | ¥850,000 | Battery large enough to charge from an outlet (typically 40–80 km electric range), plus a gasoline engine for longer trips. Examples: Toyota Prius Prime, Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV. |
| Kei EV | ¥580,000 | A BEV within the kei vehicle class. Lower cap than standard BEV because kei cars are cheaper — the subsidy is sized proportionately. Examples: Nissan Sakura, Mitsubishi eK X EV. |
| FCV (Fuel Cell Vehicle) | ¥1,500,000 | Runs on hydrogen; the fuel cell converts it to electricity on-board, emitting only water vapor. Highest cap because FCVs are expensive and hydrogen is a national strategic priority. Example: Toyota Mirai. |
| Standard HEV (non-plug-in hybrid) | ¥0 | Combustion engine assisted by an electric motor recharged only through regenerative braking — cannot be plugged in. Considered mature/mainstream in Japan and needs no incentive. Example: base Toyota Prius. |

Local subsidies (e.g. Tokyo, Saitama) can stack on top of the national subsidy. Confirm current-year amounts and eligibility at the Next Generation Vehicle Promotion Center before counting on them — budgets can run out mid-year.

## Financing as a Foreigner

US credit history does not transfer to Japan. Japanese lenders assess foreigners based on visa type and remaining duration, employer and income stability, and whether a guarantor is available.

| Loan type | Typical rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bank loan (MUFG, SMBC, regional banks, JA) | 1–4% | Lower rates, stricter screening |
| Suruga Bank (explicitly lends to non-PR foreigners) | ~6–14.5% depending on profile | More accessible for foreigners without PR |
| Dealer/captive finance (Toyota Finance, Honda Finance, JACCS) | 2–8% | Easier to access; dealer wants the sale |
| In-house used-car financing | Up to ~12% | Some offer 0% with a guarantor |

Permanent residency is not required to get a car loan. Spouse and work visas are accepted by many lenders. A Japanese-national spouse or family member as **guarantor** significantly improves approval odds and rates. Loan terms are often capped to visa remaining validity unless a guarantor or PR is in place.

Paying in full by bank transfer is common and the simplest route if cash flow allows.

**Subscription/leasing alternative:** Toyota's KINTO subscription bundles the car, taxes, insurance, and maintenance into one monthly fee — attractive for newcomers who want to avoid shaken and insurance admin. Monthly rental services (e.g. Monthly GO in Kanto) can bridge the gap before buying. Total cost of subscription is typically higher than buying.

## The Buying Process

### Paperwork required for a foreigner

- **Residence card (在留カード)** — proof of legal residence in Japan.
- **Certificate of residence (住民票 / jūminhyō)** from your city office (valid within ~3 months).
- **Registered personal seal (実印/jitsuin) + seal certificate (印鑑証明書)** — required for standard (white-plate) cars. The seal must match your residence card name exactly, including middle name and capitalization. Foreigners can register a seal in romaji, kanji, or katakana depending on the municipality; some lenders accept an embassy-issued signature certificate instead. Kei cars typically require only a signature.
- **Parking certificate (車庫証明)** from the local police station.
- **Japanese driver's license** — or an International Driving Permit (valid up to 1 year) while converting a foreign license via *gaimen kirikae*. Begin the conversion process early.

**Name consistency is critical.** Every document — contract, registration, insurance, seal — must show your name identically to the residence card. Discrepancies cause friction at title transfer and resale.

### Timeline from purchase to driving

Unlike the US where same-day drive-off is common, budget 1–3 weeks:

1. Obtain jūminhyō and register personal seal at the city office (same day).
2. Secure an off-street parking space and obtain the shako shōmei (~1 week via police).
3. Dealer handles title transfer, registration, and plates at the Land Transport Office.

New cars ordered to spec take longer; March orders can face significant delivery delays.

### Where to buy

- **Franchised brand dealers** — Toyota, Honda, Nissan dealer networks. Haggling is more muted than in the US; consensus is ~10% discount is achievable on price, with more room on accessories, trade-in, and financing terms.
- **Foreigner-friendly dealers** — English/Chinese-speaking dealers (e.g. Soukyo, KMC, Genki Cars) can handle shako shōmei, registration, and financing paperwork on your behalf.

**Best timing:** March (fiscal year-end) and September (mid-year) offer the most discounts. Avoid March if possible — the registration rush causes delivery delays and thinner inventory.

## Practical Considerations

- **Sort parking before buying.** You cannot register a standard car without the shako shōmei. Monthly parking in suburban Kanto runs roughly ¥8,000–¥15,000+.
- **Buy with fresh shaken** to avoid an immediate ¥100,000+ bill, or negotiate the price down to reflect remaining shaken.
- **Voluntary insurance (*nin'i hoken*) is separate from jibaiseki.** Compulsory *jibaiseki* covers injury to third parties only. Voluntary insurance covers your own vehicle damage, broader liability, and more. A zero Japanese no-claims history means higher initial premiums — budget ¥50,000–¥70,000/year.
- **Get an ETC card** for highway tolls — cash lanes are increasingly rare.
- **Watch for the 13-year tax surcharge** — older gasoline cars pay ~15% more in automobile tax and higher weight tax at shaken.
- **CEV subsidies change annually** and can run out mid-year. Verify current amounts before planning a BEV/PHEV purchase around them.
